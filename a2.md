# Question 1: Half-Integrality of Vertex Cover

Recall that in the vertex cover problem, we are given a graph
$G = (V,E)$ and the goal is to find the smallest subset of vertices $C$
such that every edge is incident to a vertex in $C$. Here's the LP

$$\begin{align*}
\label{a2-vc-lp}
\text{minimize} \quad & \sum_v x_v\\
\text{subject to} \quad & x_u + x_v \geq 1 \quad  && \forall (u,v) \in E\\
& x \geq 0
\end{align*}$$

On general graphs, vertex cover is NP-hard and admits a 2 approximation
via LP rounding, while on bipartite graphs, there is an exact LP
rounding algorithm. In fact, in [Tutorial 8 Exercise 6](#ex-8-6), we
showed that there is an optimal solution to the vertex cover LP on
bipartite graphs that is integral.

The goal of this question is to use the existence of integral optimal
solutions to the vertex cover LP on bipartite graphs to show that on
every graph, there exists an optimal solution $x^*$ to the vertex cover
LP that is *half-integral*, that is, $x^*_v$ is either 0, 1/2, or 1 for
every vertex $v$.

## Reduction to Bipartite Graphs

Given a graph $G = (V,E)$, we construct a bipartite graph
$H = (V_1 \cup V_2, F)$ as follows:

- for each vertex $v \in V$, we create two copies: $v_1$ in $V_1$ and
  $v_2$ in $V_2$.
- for each edge $(u,v) \in E$, we create two copies in $F$: $(u_1,v_2)$
  and $(u_2,v_1)$.

See @a2-vc-example for an illustration.

:::: {figure label=a2-vc-example}

::: {image width=75%} ./a2-vc-example.png

:::

Illustration of the reduction.

::::

$$\begin{align*}
\label{a2-vc-bip-lp}
\text{minimize} \quad & \sum_{v_1 \in V_1} y_{v_1} + \sum_{v_2 \in V_2} y_{v_2}\\
\text{subject to} \quad & y_{v_1} + y_{v_2} \geq 1 \quad  && \forall (v_1,v_2) \in F\\
& y \geq 0 \quad
\end{align*}$$

# Question 2: Tree Domination

## Problem Definition

An instance of the *Tree Domination Problem* consists of a rooted tree
$T = (V,E)$ with vertex costs $c_v$ and root vertex $r$. We say that
vertex $u$ *dominates* $v$ if $u$ is an ancestor of $v$. A subset $S$ of
$V$ is *dominating* if every leaf vertex $v$ of $T$ is dominated by some
vertex in $S$. The goal is to find a dominating set with the minimum
total cost.

### Application: Buying Myki commuter passes

Suppose you have your commute schedule for the year planned out in great
detail, specifying exactly which days you will need to take public
transport. Moreover, suppose Myki offers daily passes, weekly passes,
monthly passes and annual passes. A daily pass is the cheapest but is
only valid for one day, a weekly pass is valid from Monday to Sunday, a
monthly pass is valid for a specific month, and the annual pass is valid
for the entire year. Your goal is to compute which passes to buy so as
to minimize your total public transport cost.

We can model this as a Tree Domination problem with 4 levels of
vertices. The leaves correspond to the days you need to take public
transport. Buying a leaf corresponds to buying a daily pass that is
valid for just that day. The vertices at the next level correspond to
weekly passes. One more level up correspond to monthly passes and the
highest level is the root which corresponds to the annual pass.

:::: {figure}

::: {image width=90%} ./a2-myki-passes.png

:::

In this example, the optimal solution is to buy daily passes. However,
if Week 1 has 6 commute days, then it's optimal to buy the weekly pass
for Week 1 and daily passes for the rest of the commute days.

## Task 1.1

(a):

- Construction of $y$ \[1 mark\]. The correct transformation is to set
  $y_{v_1},y_{v_2} = x^*_v$ where $x^*$ is the optimal solution to the
  LP for $G$.
- Feasibility of $y$ \[0.5 marks\]. Nonnegativity of $y$ follows from
  nonnegativity of $x$. Consider an edge $(v_1, v_2)$ of $H$. It
  corresponds to an edge $(u,v)$ of $G$ where $u = v_1$ and $v= v_2$.
  Thus, $y_{v_1} + y_{v_2} = x_u + x_v \geq 1$. Thus, the edge
  constraints are satisfied as well.
- Optimality and half-integrality \[0.5 marks\]. Since $y$ is a feasible
  solution, we have
  $\operatorname{OPT}_H \leq \sum_{v \in V_1 \cup V_2} y_v = 2\sum_{v \in V} x^*_v = 2 \operatorname{OPT}_G$.

(b):

- Construction of $x^*$ \[1 mark\]. Let $y^*$ be an integral optimal
  solution to the LP for $H$. Define
  $x^*_v = \frac{y^*_{v_1} + y^*_{v_2}}{2}$ for each vertex $v \in V$.
- Feasibility of $x^*$ \[0.5 marks\]. Nonnegativity of $x^*$ follows
  from nonnegativity of $y$. Consider an edge $(u,v)$ of $G$. We have
  $x^*_u + x^*_v = \frac{y^*_{u_1} + y^*_{u_2}}{2} + \frac{y^*_{v_1} + y^*_{v_2}}{2} \geq 1/2 + 1/2 = 1$,
  where the inequality follows from feasibility of $y^*$. Thus, the edge
  constraints are satisfied as well.
- Optimality and half-integrality \[0.5 marks\]. Half-integrality
  follows from the fact that $y^*_v \in \{0,1\}$ for every $v \in H$.
  Optimality follows from the fact that
  $\sum_{v \in V} x^*_v = \sum_{v \in V} \frac{y^*_{v_1} + y^*_{v_2}}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{v \in V_1 \cup V_2} y^*_v = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{OPT}_H \leq \operatorname{OPT}_G$
  by part (a)

## Task 2.1

\(a\) Let $L$ be the set of leaves. For each leaf $\ell \in L$, define
$P(\ell)$ to be the vertices on the path from $\ell$ to the root. Note
that $P(\ell)$ is exactly the set of ancestors of $\ell$. For each
vertex $v$ of the tree, we have a variable $x_v$ which indicates whether
it is part of the dominating set.

$$\begin{equation}
\text{minimize} \quad & \sum_v x_v\\
\text{subject to} \quad & \sum_{v \in P(\ell)} x_v \geq 1 \quad  && \forall \ell \in L\\
& x \geq 0
\end{equation}$$

\(b\) Given a dominating set $S$, define $x_v = 1$ for $v \in S$ and
$x_v = 0$ otherwise. The covering constraints are satisfied since
$P(\ell)$ is exactly the set of ancestors of $\ell$ and a dominating set
contains at least one ancestor.

\(c\) Let $n$ be the number of vertices in $T$. The number of variables
is $n$ and the number of constraints is the number of leaves which is at
most $n$. Thus, the size of the LP is polynomial and can be solved by
the Ellipsoid Algorithm in polynomial time.

\(a\) For each leaf $\ell \in L$, we introduce a dual variable $y_\ell$.

$$\begin{equation}
\text{maximize} \quad & \sum_{\ell \in L} y_\ell\\
\text{subject to} \quad & \sum_{\ell : P(\ell) \ni v} y_\ell \geq c_v \quad  && \forall v \in T\\
& x \geq 0
\end{equation}$$

\(b\) Any dual-raising procedure which has the following form is fine:

- Initialize $S = \emptyset$ and $y=0$
- While $S$ is not a dominating set:
  - Increase $y_\ell$ for some set of leaves that are not dominated by
    $S$
  - When a dual constraint is tight for some vertex $v$: add $v$ to $S$

The key idea is that one also has to prune $S$ by removing vertices
whose ancestors are also in $S$. In the lecture, I explicitly said that
for the shortest path problem, we cannot simply return all tight edges.
This is also reinforced in Tutorial 10.

Note that it is not enough to only pick the closest vertex to the root
that is tight when a dual constraint becomes tight.

A concrete counterexample is a 2 level tree where the root has cost 1.5
and has 2 leaves, each of cost $1$. Suppose in the first iteration, we
raise the dual variable of the first leaf until the leaf itself becomes
tight. Then in the second iteration, we raise the dual variable of the
second leaf to 0.5 and the root becomes tight. The first leaf
contributes towards itself and the root and thus if we pick both of
them, dual complementary slackness is not satisfied.

\(c\) and (d) These two are essentially the standard arguments.

\(c\) We start with a feasible dual solution and stop increasing dual
variables once one of their constraints is tight.

\(d\) We only pick tight vertices and since we do not decrease dual
variables, tight vertices remain tight.

\(e\) Dual complementary slackness is satisfied since we explicitly
ensure that our pruned solution do not contain two vertices such that
one is an ancestor of another.

Comment: What we are looking for here is an explicit argument that
pruning ensures no two vertices are ancestors of each other. It is not
enough to simply say that dual complementary slackness follows from a
minimally feasible solution.

## Task 2.3

Comment: The key idea here is to use a similar approach as for the min
$(s,t)$-cut problem, except that now the LP values are on vertices not
edges. The only other deterministic rounding algorithm discussed in
lectures is to round up variables that are above some fixed threshold.
However, to guarantee feasibiilty, the threshold needs to be at most
$1/h$ where $h$ is the height of tree, which yields an $h$-approximation
and thus such an algorithm can be far from optimal.

\(a\) Let $x^*$ be an optimal LP solution. For each vertex $v$, define
its distance from the root $r$ to be $d(v) = \sum_{v \in P^-(v)} x^*_v$
where \$P\^-(v) is the set of vertices on the path from $v$ to the root,
excluding $v$, and including the root. Note that $d(r) = 0$ since
$P^-(r) = \emptyset$.

For each $\Theta \in [0,1)$, define
$S(\Theta) = \{v : d(v) \leq \Theta < d(v) + x^*_v\}$. Intuitively, if
one draws a ball of radius $\Theta$, $S(\Theta)$ is the set of vertices
that straddle the boundary of the ball. For example, if $x^*_r = 1/2$,
then $S(\Theta) = \{r\}$ for $\Theta < 1/2$ and $S(1/2)$ is the root's
children.

The randomized algorithm is to pick $\Theta \in [0,1)$ uniformly at
random and return $S(\Theta)$.

\(b\) We need to show that for every $\Theta \in [0,1)$, $S(\Theta)$ is
a dominating set. Consider a leaf $\ell$ and $\Theta \in [0,1)$. We will
show that $S(\Theta)$ contains a vertex dominating $\ell$. The LP
constraints ensure that
$d(\ell) + x^*_v = \sum_{v \in P(\ell)} x^*_v \geq 1$. Since
$0 \leq \Theta < 1$, we have that
$d(r) \leq \Theta < d(\ell) + x^*_\ell$ and so there is a vertex
$v \in P(\ell)$ such that $d(v) \leq \Theta < d(v) + x^*_v$. Therefore,
$S(\Theta)$ contains a vertex dominating $\ell$.

\(c\) We have that $v$ is in $S(\Theta)$ if and only if
$d(v) \leq \Theta < d(v) + x^*_v$. Since $\Theta$ is chosen uniformly at
random from $[0,1)$, the probability that $v$ is in $S(\Theta)$ is equal
to $d(v) + x^*_v - d(v) = x^*_v$. Thus, by linearity of expectation, the
expected cost of the algorithm is $\sum_v c_vx^*_v$, which is the
optimal LP cost and is thus at most the optimal integral cost.

\(d\) Observe that there are only a finite number of possibilities of
the output $S(\Theta)$. A weak but sufficient upper bound is that
$S(\Theta) \leq 2^n$, since $S(\Theta)$ is a subset of the vertices of
$T$. Moreover, for each $\Theta$, the cost of $S(\Theta)$ is at least
the optimal integral solution which in turn is the expected cost of
$S(\Theta)$, we get that in fact $S(\Theta)$ is optimal for every
$\Theta$. Thus, we can simply output $S(0)$, for example.

Comment: For full bonus marks, your solution needs to account for the
fact that $\Theta$ is drawn from a continuous interval which contain an

exists $\Theta$ such that the cost of $S(\Theta)$ is more than the
